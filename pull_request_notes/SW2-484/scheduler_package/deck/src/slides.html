01

What the scheduler does

A robotic parking garage, the planner that runs it, and one plan built step by step.

The domain

A robotic parking garage

Cars never move on their own. Every car sits on a tray, and every tray move needs an AGV to drive under it, lift it, carry it and lower it.

AGV

Automated guided vehicle. Low, drives under trays, lifts and carries one. AGV-1, AGV-2, …

Tray

A platform with legs that holds a car or stands empty. Its front overdrive plate (ODP) retracts.

Bay

Where customers drop off and pick up cars. Switches between insert and retrieve modes.

Stalls and waypoints

Parking stalls (P) store trays; waypoints (W) are aisle positions the AGVs drive through.

Chargers (VECS)

Volley EV charging stations: charge AGVs, and the cars parked on trays.

Lifts (VRC)

Vertical reciprocating conveyors carry an AGV, with or without a tray, between floors.

Keep this concrete: a customer asks for their car; the system must bring the tray holding that car into a bay. AGVs are the only things that move. Trays are passive obstacles: a tray parked in the way must be moved first. Bays, chargers and lifts are resources with their own states and timing.
The domain as a graph

A real garage layout, and why moves interact

The layout file turns the garage into a graph: nodes are places, edges are where an AGV may drive.

One tray per node. Storage is dense, so reaching one tray often means moving others first.

This is the real Pecos geometry. Step through: the root tray R is boxed in by K and T20. This is the core difficulty of the domain: a simple request ("bring me R") creates extra work that depends on routes.
Where the scheduler sits

Dispatch asks, the scheduler plans, the fleet executes

Dispatch is the executive: it owns the job queue and the running schedule. Every 850 ms it may call the planner. Today it only calls when no job is running, so each plan starts from an idle fleet; Part 4 comes back to this. The scheduler is a pure function from (garage snapshot, jobs) to an action graph. It never talks to robots.
The scheduler's job

Jobs in, an ordered graph of actions out

Input: goals

"The car on T17 is handed over in a bay." A job states an outcome, not the moves. Which bay, which empty tray, which AGV and which route are the planner's choice.

Output: an order, not a timetable

Each action names one machine and the actions it must wait for. Robots run at whatever speed they run; the order keeps them safe.

This is the whole contract in one picture. Lanes are machines. Arrows are "must finish before". The amber arrows cross lanes: those are what make the plan safe without clocks. Times are not part of the output; Dispatch releases an action as soon as all its parents are done.
How it is designed

Five stages inside one Plan() call

1

Job expansion

What must happen?
Each job becomes events: "bay to retrieve", "move T17 into B21", destinations still blank.
2

Tray assignment

Which tray goes where, and what is in the way?
Fills blanks (empty tray, parking spot), adds moves for blocking trays.
3

AGV assignment

Which AGV moves each tray?
Matches AGVs to tray moves, adds drive, lift and lower events.
4

Motion planning

Which path, and when, without collisions?
CHUGG plans timed paths for every AGV, one event at a time.
5

Action graph

What order must the robots respect?
Turns timed paths into the dependency graph Dispatch runs.

timed motions
action DAG

Stages 1-3 are task planning and live in the scheduler package: 78% of its code. Stage 4 is a separate library (motion_planner). This refactor is about stages 2 and 3.

The event DAG is the key data structure: a graph of intended events (tray moves, AGV moves, bay transitions) with "must happen before" edges. Each task-planning stage reads it and edits it in place. That in-place editing is also the root of the coupling we want to fix.

Top: the garage. Bottom: the event DAG as each stage writes it, then the plan played back.

Walk through the 13 steps. Points to stress: expansion writes intent with blanks; tray assignment fills the blanks and discovers the blocker T15; AGV assignment wraps each tray move in drive, lift, carry, lower; then the plan plays. Note that one simple request became three tray moves.
Stage 2 · tray assignment

Blocking trays create extra moves

The root tray R is boxed in. Tray assignment finds a route, finds what stands on it, and picks a free stall for each blocker.

Which route and which stall are choices: a better solver could make fewer or shorter moves.

This is the algorithm inside DoTrayAssignments, 560 lines today. The ticket's first interface wraps exactly this decision: given the event DAG and the garage, fill the blanks and add the blocker moves.
Stage 3 · AGV assignment

Matching AGVs to tray moves

A cost per (AGV, tray move) pair, then a bottleneck matching: minimise the worst pair, not the total.

The cost function and the matching rule are both policies a different solver could change.

This is DoAgvAssignments, 365 lines. Step 9 is the interesting one: the matching with the cheaper total loses because its worst pair is larger. Whether that is the right objective is exactly the kind of question benchmarks should answer, which is why the ticket asks for a swappable interface plus a benchmark set.
Stage 4 · motion planning

CHUGG plans one AGV at a time

CHUGG takes a multi-agent path-finding problem with precedence constraints (MAPF-PC) as input; its algorithm is prioritized planning with SIPP (safe-interval path planning) for each AGV.

Prioritized planning: plan events in the DAG's order; each planned motion becomes an obstacle in time for everyone after it. It is fast, but earlier events never yield. That is why the order the scheduler hands over (its edges and job order) steers the quality of the result. Motion planning is out of scope for this ticket.
Stage 5 · action graph

Why the output is an order, not a timetable

Real robots are late. A timetable breaks the moment one AGV is delayed. An action dependency graph keeps only the orders that matter for safety ("AGV-3 must leave C before AGV-1 enters"), so a delay simply makes the others wait.
Part 1 recap

What to keep in mind

The scheduler is a planner, not a controller. It turns jobs and a garage snapshot into an action graph; Dispatch runs it.

Requests create work. Dense storage means blockers, so the set of moves depends on routes and choices.

Five stages, one shared workspace. Stages 1-3 edit one event DAG in place; stage 4 plans paths; stage 5 builds the order.

The decisions in stages 2 and 3 are policies. Better ones exist, but today they cannot be swapped or measured.

02

What we want to address, and why

The Jira initiative, where the code stands, and what makes it hard to change.

Jira · Scheduler Core Refactor

What the ticket asks for

Objective. Move tray-move planning and AGV assignment out of large Scheduler methods and behind generic, swappable interfaces. Defaults must reproduce today's behaviour.

Definition of Done

  • Two generic interfaces: tray assignment and AGV assignment
  • Two behaviour-equivalent default implementations
  • 10+ real-site benchmark scenarios per solver, with saved baselines
  • Every assignment call site routed through the interfaces; old paths removed

Out of scope: changes to CHUGG/SIPP motion planning, a new optimal solver, adding to active schedules.

SW2-769 Done

Scheduler package reorganization.

SW2-484 In progress

Tray solver interface. The natural first implementation ticket.

SW2-487 To do

Tray benchmark set. Can start once baseline capture is deterministic.

No AGV-assignment child ticket exists yet; the plan needs one.

The ticket is deliberately narrow: no new algorithm, no motion-planning change. It is an enabling refactor. The payoff is that the next person who wants to try a better tray or AGV solver can do it behind an interface and prove it with numbers.

The issues

What makes the planning core hard to change

Hidden inputs and outputs

The assignment methods read and write the event DAG, the working garage state and member maps in place. Nothing says what a call needs or changes.

Untestable in isolation

Testing tray assignment means building a whole Scheduler. There is no unit to test, and no seam to swap.

Shared helpers

The same helpers (headings, blockers, empty-tray choice) serve job expansion. Moving them naively breaks callers or adds back-pointers.

Inconsistent errors

BoolStringPair, std::optional and silent false returns mix; some failures lose their reason.

One concept, many names

gg_, table_, CollisionEntityId, "disabled": names that hide what things are and slow down every reader.

No yardstick

No benchmark set, so no way to tell whether a change makes plans better, worse or slower.

Red items are what the interfaces fix directly. Amber items are what make the work safe and reviewable: one error type, clear names, and numbers to compare against.

Why now

Why this is worth doing

Better plans become possible

Tray and AGV choices decide how many moves a retrieve needs and how long customers wait. Interfaces let us try better policies without rewriting the scheduler.

Changes become measurable

A benchmark set on real layouts turns "seems better" into numbers: moves, makespan, planning time.

Safer day-to-day work

Small pure functions with unit tests, one error type, explicit inputs: fixes stop reaching into unrelated stages.

Faster onboarding

Folders that match the pipeline and names that say what things are. New people find the stage they need.

03

The proposed changes

Interfaces, layout, names and errors, delivered in small steps that keep behaviour identical.

Principles

Four rules for the whole refactor

1 · Same behaviour first

The default solvers reproduce today exactly: same assignments, same event DAG after reduction, byte-identical responses on the baseline fixtures. Improvements come later, behind the interfaces.

2 · Small mechanical steps

Each pull request does one kind of change (move, rename, extract) and is checked by parity tests, not by reading 500-line diffs.

3 · Rename only where we own the blast radius

Names change in scheduler and planner_core only. common_ros is used by too many modules; its names are translated at the edge instead.

4 · Folders now, packages when proven

Stage folders with one build target each inside scheduler. A stage becomes its own package only once it has a second user and a stable API.

Rule 1 is the most important: it is what makes a large refactor reviewable. If a step changes a plan, it is a bug in the step, by definition.
Target architecture

Plan() becomes an orchestrator of swappable stages

The orchestrator keeps the order of stages and owns the solver objects. Each assignment stage gets a small interface with one call. Shared services that today are Scheduler members (gg_, table_) become an explicit GarageServices object passed in.
The tray interface

Read-only input, a returned result, one place that applies it

namespace volley::task_planning::tray_assignment {
struct Input {          // read-only view
  const DiscreteGarageState& initial_state;
  const EventDag& event_dag;
};
struct Result {         // an edit log
  std::vector<TrayFill> fills;   // blanks filled
  std::vector<DagEdit>  edits;   // events, edges
  /* first event per tray, for AGV assignment */
};
class Solver {
 public:
  virtual ~Solver() = default;
  [[nodiscard]] virtual
  stdx::Expected<Result> Solve(const Input&) const = 0;
};
}
// the only writer of the plan context
ApplyTrayAssignment(plan_ctx, result);

Solve cannot touch the plan

It sees a const view and returns what it decided. Two solvers can run on the same input and be compared.

The result is inspectable

Fills and edits are data: tests assert on them directly, and a log shows exactly what a solver changed.

Replay keeps parity

ApplyTrayAssignment replays the edits in the order today's code makes them, so the event DAG comes out identical.

AGV assignment gets the same layout and error type, but edits the event DAG in place for its first version (SolveInPlace): its loop is harder to turn into a pure edit log.

This is the core design decision. Returning a result instead of mutating shared state is what makes the solver testable and swappable. The edit log is ordered to reproduce today's DAG byte for byte.

Layout

One folder per pipeline stage

scheduler/ # package name unchanged ├── CMakeLists.txt # one target per stage ├── include/scheduler/ │ ├── scheduler.hpp # Plan(): the orchestrator │ ├── plan_context.hpp # was ScheduleContext │ ├── core/ # shared, includes no stage │ ├── ros/ # messages ↔ internal types │ ├── task_planning/ │ │ ├── job_expansion/ │ │ ├── tray_assignment/ # this ticket │ │ ├── agv_assignment/ # this ticket │ │ └── validation/ │ ├── motion_planning/ │ └── action_graph/ ├── src/ # mirrors include/; private headers └── test/ # mirrors include/

Boundaries the build enforces

Each stage is a library target. A CI include check stops one stage from reaching into another's private headers.

Why not packages today?

Packages add release and ownership overhead before the APIs settle. A folder is cheap to promote later; action_graph is the first candidate.

SW2-769 already reorganized the package; the first step is to check its structure against this layout. Tests mirror the folders so each stage's tests link only that stage.
Errors and names

One error type, names that say what things are

Errors

// proposed

Values and errors travel together; [[nodiscard]] stops a failure from being ignored.

Verification

How we know nothing changed

Parity

72 active tests plus recorded DAGs and response lists on curated fixtures. Every refactor step must reproduce them.

Benchmarks (SW2-487)

≥10 real-site scenarios per solver, timed in a pinned environment, results stored with metadata: the yardstick for any future solver.

Some test layouts (tiny_lot, triple_pick_lot) are synthetic; we need to confirm which are real sites before counting them toward the ten-scenario goal.
Risks

Odd behaviours we keep on purpose, for now

A reviewer may want to "fix" these during extraction. Doing so breaks parity; each gets its own ticket and test instead.

AGV busy time is always zero

GetAgvOccupiedDuration is a stub, and the AGV matching costs depend on it.

Where the DAG is reduced

Reduction runs in two specific places; moving it earlier changes AGV matching, probably because some code reads only direct edges.

Blank fields mean something

A tray move with no destination removes the tray from the working state. Later jobs rely on it.

Vertex indices are identities

Removing an event shifts later indices, and CHUGG maps motions back by index. Never remove; never cache indices.

04

What comes next

Work the refactor makes possible, outside the current ticket.

Future · the event graph

Not every edge is a real constraint

The event DAG hands the motion planner every edge as a hard order. Only the green (causal) ones are true in every plan. Blue depend on routes chosen earlier, amber are arbitrary choices between two valid orders, red come from a conservative rule. Treating them all as hard costs parallelism.
Future · the event graph

A path from today's planner to better plans

1 Store each edge's kind

Causal, path-dependent, resource-order or conservative, plus the rule that made it. Nothing else changes; fits the refactor's interfaces.

2 Measure the cost of over-constraint

On the benchmark set, compare makespan and retrieve arrival times with all edges against causal edges only. If the gap is small, stop here.

3 Let the solver choose the free orders

Pass resource-order edges as choices to a priority-based search with precedence constraints (PBS-PC); keep causal edges hard.

4 Give the planner an objective

Start with bay choice and job order, scored by predicted retrieve waiting time, behind the new solver interfaces.

Open questions before step 3: what the primary objective is (retrieve wait, throughput, a mix), how long a planning call may take, and whether bay and patron action durations can be bounded.
Future · Dispatch

Planning while the fleet is moving

Today Dispatch calls the planner only when nothing is running, so a new job waits for the whole current schedule. Overlapping planning needs the scheduler to keep the running actions unchanged and plan around them, with time-bounded reservations instead of reserving whole routes. Related Dispatch items: no timeout on a lost plan response, and a CLEAN_GARAGE job that blocks jobs queued behind it.
Future · motion planning

The same interface idea for the motion planner

Harder than the task-planning interfaces because CHUGG's inputs are tied to event vertex indices and the collision table. A MotionProblem / MotionPlan pair lets other planners (conflict-based search, PBS) be tried, including ones that support only part of the problem.
